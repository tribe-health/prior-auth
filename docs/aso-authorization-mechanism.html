The argument chain

This is the core of it — the thing that makes the product work and that no generic prior-auth vendor knows.

The trap in the criteria

Lumbar fusion is not authorized for back pain. Back pain is why most patients need it. The MRI in a back-pain patient typically shows an annular tear and disc degeneration — neither of which authorizes anything.

They also require pre-existing instability on flexion-extension, 3–5 mm translation. As you put it: "Nobody has that. That's why they wrote the criteria that way."

The route through is not to argue harder about back pain. It's to establish a different chain entirely:

1
MRI reports moderate or severe foraminal stenosis at the operative level. Not mild. This single word governs everything downstream.
↓
2
Neurologic findings correlate to the exiting nerve root at that specific foramen — motor weakness 4/5 in the muscle group that root supplies, plus dermatomal sensory change.
↓
3
Addressing foraminal stenosis requires wide decompression — not central laminectomy, but laterally into the lateral recess and the foramen itself.
↓
4
That decompression resects greater than 50% of the facet joints bilaterally.
↓
5
Which produces iatrogenic instability — and iatrogenic instability necessitating fusion is in the criteria for every carrier.

The instability requirement gets satisfied not by finding it on a flexion-extension film, but by demonstrating that the indicated decompression will create it. That's the mechanism, and it's the thing the system has to build every argument around.

The radiology chokepoint

You
"Trying to set up meetings with radiologists so they quit putting the word 'mild' on the foraminal stenosis report."

One adjective in a radiology report decides whether a case is authorizable. That is the single highest-leverage variable in the entire pipeline, and it's produced by someone who has no idea what they're deciding.

What the system does about it

  1. Flags it at intake. Imaging says "mild" at the operative level and the case needs "moderate" — that surfaces before submission, not after denial.
  2. Tracks it by radiologist and by facility. Over sixty cases you'll know exactly which readers systematically under-grade foraminal stenosis. That turns your radiologist conversations from an impression into a dataset.
  3. Routes the remediable ones. Where the images support a higher grade than the report states, the paths are a re-read request, a formal addendum, or an independent second read — each documented as a legitimate clinical disagreement about interpretation.
The line to hold here

Asking a radiologist to change a grade because you need it for authorization is a different act from asking for a re-read because you believe the images show more than the report captured. The first is pressure on a colleague to alter a clinical finding. The second is ordinary medical practice.

The system should only ever generate the second — a re-read request stating what the surgeon observes on the images, with the question left open. That distinction protects you and it also produces a better document.

The reviewer — and a change I'd propose

You
"The initial request goes to some internal medicine doctor who doesn't know what he's looking at… I want the name and medical license number of that guy, because I'm going to threaten him with a board complaint if he doesn't do what I ask."

The instinct is right. The execution I'd change — and I think the version I'm proposing is materially stronger, not softer.

Why the personal threat is the weaker weapon

Threatening an individual physician with a board complaint to influence a clinical determination can be characterized as coercion of a medical decision-maker. You may never be challenged on it. But if you ever are — in an arbitration, in a deposition, in a payer's counter-complaint — that sentence does more damage to you than the denial did.

What I'd do instead, which gets the same result

Texas law already gives you the lever. Utilization review affecting Texas patients is regulated: the UR agent must be registered with TDI, and physicians conducting it are subject to Texas licensure and specialty-match requirements. Under HB 3459, a peer review preceding an adverse determination must be conducted by a Texas-licensed physician of the same or similar specialty.

So capture the reviewer's identity — but use it differently:

  1. Record name, credentials, and specialty from every determination letter. This is information you are entitled to.
  2. Check it automatically against the Texas Medical Board licensure database and against the specialty requirement.
  3. Where it fails — an out-of-state reviewer, or an internist reviewing a spine fusion — that is a documented regulatory violation, and it goes to TDI as a compliance complaint about the utilization review agent, not as a threat to a person.
  4. Where it passes, you still have a record: which reviewers deny at what rate, and whether the same names recur across your denials.
Why this is the stronger position

A board complaint against an individual is a threat you have to be willing to carry out, and it reads as personal. A pattern of same-specialty violations documented across twenty cases is a regulatory filing against the company that they cannot dismiss, that puts their TDI registration in play, and that costs you nothing to accumulate because the system builds it automatically.

It also scales. One board complaint pressures one reviewer once. A compliance file pressures the vendor on every case you will ever send them.

What goes in the first submission

You said the regulator should be named in the initial document. I pushed back on that previously; having heard the mechanism, I'd partly reverse. A factual procedural notice — not a threat — is appropriate and signals that the file will be handled properly:

Proposed standing paragraph · TDI-regulated plans
This request is submitted under a plan regulated by the Texas Department of Insurance. Any adverse determination is subject to the peer review requirements of the Texas Insurance Code, including review by a Texas-licensed physician of the same or similar specialty. Please include the reviewing physician's name, credentials, and specialty with any determination.

That last sentence does the work. Asking for the reviewer's identity as a routine procedural matter is entirely proper, gets you the data without a confrontation, and tells them the file is being handled by someone who knows the rules.

The risk I have to raise

Read this one carefully

You said: "Most of these patients, I don't really even need to do the laminectomy, but I'm trying to fuse them, so I'm presenting to them that I'm going to do a laminectomy."

I think I know what you mean, and the charitable reading is almost certainly the correct one: the decompression is genuinely part of the operation you perform, and what you're saying is that decompression alone wouldn't be your chosen treatment — the fusion is the operation. A wide foraminal decompression really does destabilize, and that's a standard, defensible surgical argument.

But that sentence, as spoken, describes representing a planned procedure to obtain authorization for a different one. In a deposition, an audit, or an OIG review, it reads as a false statement to a payer. And if a letter this system generates asserts a wide decompression that the operative report doesn't document, the mismatch is discoverable — payers audit for exactly that.

Which is why I'm building one specific control

The system will reconcile every authorization request against the operative report and flag divergence — planned levels versus performed, planned decompression extent versus documented, authorized CPTs versus billed.

Read one way that's a compliance burden. Read correctly, it's the thing that protects you: if the wide decompression is performed and the operative report documents it, the argument in the letter was true and you have contemporaneous proof. That reconciliation record is the single best defense you could have if anyone ever questions the pattern.

The system either protects you here or it convicts you. The difference is whether the letter describes what actually happens in the OR — and that's a design decision I need you to make with your eyes open, not one I'll make quietly.
