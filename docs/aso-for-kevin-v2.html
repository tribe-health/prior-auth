The insurer isn't trying to win the denial. They're trying to make the denial expensive enough that some fraction of doctors give up. You don't give up — you pay in unbillable hours instead. The software's job is to make the fight cheap, not to make it winnable.

The one that matters more when you're on a line of credit

This is the argument I'd make to a banker

A case that takes twelve days to authorize instead of seven is a case whose entire professional fee sits five days further out. Multiply that across every case in your pipeline and you are financing the insurance company's review process with your line of credit.

Compressing the authorization cycle doesn't just save hours. It pulls a chunk of revenue permanently forward — once, when the cycle shortens, and then it stays shortened. For a practice that's fine on paper but tight on cash, that one-time pull-forward is worth more than the hours.

A denial that adds thirty to forty-five days pushes a whole case past a month-end. Two or three of those in a quarter is the difference between drawing on the line and not.

Uploading isn't the shortcut version. It's the only design that survives your actual working life. You practise across systems you don't control and you're about to change the one you do. A tool that only works inside one EHR is a tool that works for you a few months a year.

What this buys

The tool works identically whether the records came from AdvancedMD, eClinicalWorks, Centricity, a fax from an outside pain clinic, or a PDF someone printed and scanned. It doesn't know and doesn't care.

It also means an MSO switching you to their EHR is a non-event. You keep working the same way the next morning.

Before you sign the MSO agreement

I'm not your lawyer and you should have one read this. But there are four specific things in MSO contracts that would directly affect what we're discussing, and they're easier to negotiate before signing than after.

  1. Who owns the clinical data and can you export it? If the MSO's EHR holds your records and the contract restricts export, the upload workflow gets harder and leaving gets much harder.
  2. Does the MSO claim rights over ancillary revenue or services? Many MSO agreements sweep in any revenue-generating service associated with the practice. If you later want a patient-facing authorization service, that clause decides whether it's yours.
  3. Who controls prior authorization and revenue cycle? Some MSOs take this over entirely — which might solve your problem outright and make this whole project unnecessary. Ask them directly what their denial and appeal process is, who does it, and what their overturn rate is. If they're good at it, that's the cheapest possible answer.
  4. Non-compete and software restrictions. Whether you can use outside tools in their environment at all.
The question worth asking them first

"What happens when one of my fusions gets denied? Who writes the appeal, who takes the peer-to-peer, and what percentage do you overturn?"

If the MSO has a real answer, take it and we don't build anything. If they hedge, you've learned that this stays your problem and you've learned it before signing rather than after.

Sheer Health, and "I need the patients to sign up"

Your patient paying just under $100 a month for a service that fights his denial for him is the most interesting fact in your message. It's proof that patients will pay for this — which is not obvious and is worth a lot.

But there are two very different things you might mean by wanting patients to sign up, and they carry completely different risk.

Reading one — you refer patients to an existing service

Low risk · testable this month · costs you nothing

You tell patients facing a denial that this service exists, they engage it directly, and they pay it. You have no financial relationship with it.

This offloads real work at zero cost to you, and you can start Monday. I'd test it on your next five denials before we build anything. If it works, some of your problem is already solved and the software gets smaller. If it doesn't, you'll know exactly where it falls short — which tells us what to build.

Keep it clean: mention it as one option among others, don't require it, don't take anything from them, and document that the patient chose it.

Reading two — you build or own a service and charge your patients

This one has real problems and I'd want a healthcare attorney before a line of code

You may already be contractually obligated to do this work. Participating-provider agreements generally make obtaining prior authorization part of the provider's responsibility. Charging a patient separately for something your payer contract already requires of you can breach that contract, and payers do enforce it.

Steering your own patients to a service you profit from is a different legal question than referring them to an independent one. The moment you have a financial interest, self-referral and inducement rules come into view. Sheer Health works partly because it isn't the surgeon's company.

And the incentive reads badly. A patient being told by their surgeon that a monthly fee to the surgeon's own service will help get their surgery approved is a sentence that could look coercive even when nothing improper is happening.

None of this makes it impossible. It makes it a business with a compliance structure, not a feature — and it's the wrong thing to attempt while cash is tight.

What I'd actually do about Sheer Health

Refer patients to it now, watch closely, and treat it as market research you're being paid to conduct. What you learn — what patients will pay, what the service does well, where it leaves the practice still holding work — is exactly what you'd need to know before anyone built a competing one.

The complaint language, briefly

Not in the first request. Nothing has gone wrong yet and the reviewer might approve. Opening adversarially costs something and gains nothing.

In the appeal, as a statement of process rather than a threat. A threat is a promise you might not keep. This isn't:

Closing paragraph · appeal letter · TDI-regulated plans only

This appeal was filed on 08/18/2026. Under Texas Insurance Code Chapter 4201, a determination is due no later than thirty calendar days from receipt. Should this appeal be denied, the patient intends to request review by an Independent Review Organization under Chapter 4202, and the IRO's determination on medical necessity is binding on the utilization review agent.

The caveat that decides whether to use it

TDI's authority — appeal timelines, IRO rights, complaints, the gold-card law — reaches only fully insured HMO, PPO and EPO plans, roughly 20% of Texans. If the patient's employer self-insures, federal ERISA law governs and TDI has no jurisdiction.

Invoking TDI at a self-funded plan's reviewer is empty, and an experienced reviewer knows it on sight. You've just told them you don't know which rules apply to them, on exactly the case where you needed credibility.

The tool knows which kind of plan it is from the eligibility check and inserts the right language, the ERISA version, or none. That's the piece that's hard to get right by hand every time.

The money-back question, short version

I researched it. One finding is immediately useful and the rest is less than you'd hope.

Use this now, regardless of software

On a preauthorization denial, the carrier or its utilization review agent pays the Independent Review Organization fee — $650 for a physician review. Not the patient, not you. External review is free to escalate to.

A TDI complaint creates real pressure — an investigator is assigned, the insurer must respond and produce its documentation, and that scrutiny often prompts reconsideration. TDI can penalize and require corrective action. It cannot award your patient money or reverse a clinical judgment call on its own.

For money actually spent — extra PT or injections done only to satisfy criteria, or a patient who self-paid to avoid waiting — Texas Insurance Code Chapters 541 and 542 allow a bad-faith action with actual damages, interest and attorney's fees. That's a lawsuit with its own economics, not a form. And it's largely unavailable on self-funded plans, where federal preemption limits the remedy to the benefit itself.

The leverage isn't recoupment. It's the record. Every overturn you can document is evidence the original denial was wrong. Accumulate them and you can pursue gold-card exemptions on services where you clear 90%, contest a rescission with the claim list in hand, and hold a payer conversation with a number instead of an impression.
