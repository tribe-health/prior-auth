About · How this works

A letter is an argument.
This is where its evidence comes from.

A surgeon decides an operation is necessary. An insurer decides whether to pay for it. Between those two decisions sits a document, and most of the work of prior authorization is assembling the evidence that document stands on. This product is about that assembly — and about never letting a claim into the letter that the record cannot support.

01 · The problem

Prior authorization is not a form. It is an evidentiary proceeding conducted by fax, in which a practice must prove that a proposed operation satisfies criteria the payer wrote, using documents the payer has never seen, to a reviewer whose name it often does not learn.

We don't actually know what piece of information the health insurer is looking for, so we send a bunch of explanations.

American Medical Association · on why prior auth is a guessing game

That sentence describes the whole failure mode. A practice that does not know the target sends everything, which buries the two facts that mattered. A practice that guesses wrong gets a denial that arrives weeks later, costs an appeal, and delays an operation for a patient in pain.

The product's answer is not to write better letters faster. It is to make the state of the evidence visible before anyone writes anything — what the policy requires, what the chart actually says, and precisely where those two disagree.

02 · Three states, not two

Most software models a requirement as met or unmet. That is one state too few, and the missing one is where the work is.

State 01 Met A dated source document satisfies the criterion. Cite it and move on.
State 02 Not met — a gap A document exists and it contradicts or falls short of the criterion. The chart says no. This must be argued.
State 03 Not documented — a void Nothing in the record addresses the criterion at all. Nothing has been disproved. This must be obtained.

A gap and a void call for opposite actions. A gap needs a surgeon to explain why the contradiction does not defeat the request. A void needs a coordinator to go get a lab result. Collapsing them into one "unmet" bucket sends the wrong person to do the wrong job, which is how a case sits for three weeks waiting on a nicotine test nobody ordered.

The distinction is not a label in the interface. It is a lookup table in the database with a foreign key, so no code path can quietly conflate the two.

03 · The gate

The workflow is ten steps, and step six is different from the other nine. Steps 01–05 assemble and analyze. Steps 07–10 exist only because a surgeon affirmed step 06.

Four affirmations, each recorded with who and when: this is the controlling policy; this is the section it must satisfy; this is the operation I intend to perform; the described levels and extent match my operative plan.

The gate is enforced in the database, not in the interface. A trigger refuses the affirmation unless the actor holds the clinical capability, and a second trigger refuses to let a letter be signed for a case that has not been affirmed. A future API route, a batch job, or somebody at a database prompt cannot route around it.

ERROR: user a0000000-… may not affirm the surgeon gate; affirm_gate is a clinical capability ERROR: case d0000000-… has not been affirmed at the surgeon gate; a letter may not be signed

The administrator is the point. Dana Whitfield can add an EMR connection, invite a surgeon, and read the audit log — and cannot affirm a gate or sign a letter. That is not an org-chart convenience. It is the safety boundary the whole role model exists to draw.

04 · Where claims come from

Every sentence in a letter of medical necessity is a claim, and every claim traces to exactly one source. There are three legitimate kinds, and the schema makes a claim declare which one it is.

Source 01 A chart document An MRI report, a lab, a therapy note. Dated, hashed, and cited to the page.
Source 02 A surgeon annotation The treating physician's clinical judgment, in his own words, attributed to him by name — never rendered as though a document said it.
Source 03 A payer criterion The rule itself, introduced the way its evidence grade permits.

The annotation is the interesting one. It is the single mechanism by which a claim enters a letter without a chart document behind it — the surgeon saying a CT myelogram demonstrates nerve-root stenosis more accurately than MRI when no document in the chart says so. That is legitimate clinical argument, and it must be possible. It must also never be disguised as a chart fact.

-- Every claim resolves to exactly one source. The XOR is what stops -- surgeon opinion from being rendered as though the chart said it. CONSTRAINT letter_claims_one_source CHECK ( num_nonnulls(document_id, annotation_id, criterion_id) = 1)

Recording a thought and arguing it are different acts, so they are different columns. A surgeon may capture a point for the record, for a peer-to-peer call, or for a future appeal without putting it in this letter.

05 · What actually gets approved The most valuable thing here, and the most dangerous.

Payer criteria arrive two ways. Some are published — a policy document with a number, a version, and a URL, hash-verified against what was retrieved. Others are derived: a surgeon watches forty authorizations and works out what actually gets approved, which the payer has never written down anywhere.

Derived criteria are how a practice stops guessing. Sharing them between surgeons is the most valuable thing this product can do — and the reason provenance is treated as load-bearing rather than descriptive.

Citable as policy Published · obtained on request A document exists. The letter may state it as the payer's own requirement, because it is.
Never citable as policy Verbal · derived · peer-shared The letter must introduce these as what they are — in this practice's experience, as reported by peer practices. Attributing one as published policy raises an error.

Derived knowledge decays, so the schema makes it decay. A peer-reviewed study documents physicians gaming prior-auth protocols until payers notice and rescind them — so a rule observed two years ago may be actively wrong today. Retrieval rank blends similarity with evidence grade and an exponential recency decay. A fresh derived criterion scores 0.64; the same rule unconfirmed for two years scores 0.0385; current published policy scores 1.0. It cannot quietly outrank the written rule.

Sharing is aggregate-only, and the boundary is structural. Federal antitrust guidance treats competitors sharing clinical information about the mode, quality, or efficiency of treatment as low risk, and sharing fee or reimbursement information as high risk. So the shared table has no money column, no fee column, no allowed-amount column — and a database event trigger refuses any future schema change that adds one. A price cannot be shared because there is nowhere to put it.

It carries no case identifier, no date finer than a quarter, and no procedure code finer than a family, with a floor of ten observations from at least two independent practices before anything crosses a practice boundary.

06 · Proving what was sent

The question after submission is not did we send it but can we prove what the payer received. A denial that says "we never got the imaging" is either true or false, and a practice should be able to say which.

Every attachment carries a page count and a content hash taken at the moment of transmission. Acknowledgement is recorded per item, so a payer that confirms 38 of 42 pages leaves four disputed rows — and those four are the worklist. The custody chain hashes each entry to the previous one, so a silent edit anywhere breaks verification from that point forward.

The same principle governs the reviewer. A denial issued by an unnamed reviewer, or by one whose specialty does not match the surgery, is a fact the practice should be able to assert with evidence — so reviewer identity, specialty match, and disclosure status are recorded fields rather than recollection.

07 · What the AI does — and does not

There are AI assistants throughout this product. They read hundreds of chart pages and find the sentence that bears on criterion 3.2. They draft. They surface what a payer approved in comparable cases. They watch published policies and notice when one changes.

They do not decide anything clinical. That is not a policy written in a prompt — a prompt is a request, not a constraint. It is enforced at two layers that do not depend on the model behaving.

01 The agent is a different principal than the surgeon An assistant acting for Dr. James is not Dr. James. In the authorization model they are separate entity types, so a rule granting the surgeon the ability to sign grants the assistant nothing. Authority must be re-granted explicitly, per action, or it does not exist.
02 Irreversible acts require a named human release Drafting is preparation. Affirming, signing, and submitting are decisions. The agent prepares and flags; a named person releases.
03 Every retrieval into a draft is logged A letter cannot be signed while a criterion that is not citable as policy sits unresolved in its retrieval log. Each one must be attributed or explicitly excluded by the surgeon first.
04 Generated interface elements carry no authority When an assistant produces a form or an approval card, that surface is a projection of what the runtime decided — it never executes a tool call or a policy decision itself.

Patient text is treated as radioactive. An embedding of clinical text is not de-identified data — published research reconstructs source text from embedding vectors, so an embedding of a chart note is itself protected health information. Each embedding model records whether it is cleared for patient data, a model may only be cleared if it runs locally or is covered by a business associate agreement, and the database refuses to store a vector of patient text against an uncleared model.

08 · Who may do what

Three roles, because two is too coarse. The split that matters is not seniority — it is between configuring the system and making a medical decision.

Access is not the variable — every role can open every screen, because a coordinator who cannot see the gate cannot understand why a case is stalled. The variable is capability, and the two clinical ones are scarce on purpose.
